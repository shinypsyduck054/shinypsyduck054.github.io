Why Not? — Roads Not Taken

The main turn-around plan leans heavily on premium decking, a possible first hire, and a Beaufort beachhead. This page interrogates the moves we didn't emphasize — staying solo, staying in Walterboro, what compounding price & process discipline does over 3–5 years, and the two specific work-schedule shapes you asked about: 5 days a week with two months off in winter vs. 4-day weeks with a standard two-week vacation. 8–5, no exceptions, in both.

What we may have missed

The original turn-around plan is biased toward growth — productize, hire, beachhead a richer market. But the real question buried in "overworked solo at $85/hr" isn't how do I grow? — it's how do I extract maximum money and maximum life from what I already have? Those are different objectives.

The frame we underplayed

A capacity-constrained solo with a waitlist already has the rarest asset in small business: pricing power. Spent on price + ruthless triage + process, it compounds quietly for 3–5 years and beats almost every "scale" path on a risk-adjusted basis — without a lease, a hire, a brand pivot, or a 48-mile commute. Staying small is a strategy, not a default.

Stay Solo, Stay in Walterboro

The main plan treats Walterboro as a handicap and Beaufort as the prize. That framing is half-right — and it's the half that gets you into a lease you don't need.

What Walterboro actually gives you

  • Owned-land overhead vs. a $3–6k/month coastal yard lease. That's $36–72k/yr that flows straight to take-home in a low-overhead shop and disappears in a waterfront one.
  • No commute, no harbormaster, no marina politics, no flood insurance, no tidal scheduling.
  • I-95 corridor is a shipping advantage, not just a customer one — every EVA decking kit you ever mail-order goes out the front door onto an interstate.
  • 10-year local reputation. You're already "the boat guy" in a 30-mile radius. That's free demand that took a decade to build and can't be re-bought at any price.
  • Below-the-radar moat. No coastal yard is going to open a Walterboro branch. Your competitive set is one shop (Juicebox) and out-of-town generalists who can't trailer for free.
  • Low cost of living. $200k in Walterboro lives like $320k in Bluffton — the dollar buys more of your actual life there.

What "stay solo" actually gives you

  • No payroll, no employer tax, no workers' comp escalation, no IC misclassification risk, no schedule built around someone else's life. Saves ~$60–90k/yr in overhead vs. a $50k W-2 hire fully loaded.
  • You stay a craftsman, not a manager. The skills that built the shop are the skills that keep running it.
  • No HR drama, no turnover, no training tax, no "what do I do when they don't show up Monday?"
  • Variable cost = zero. Slow week? Your overhead doesn't care. Try that with a coastal lease and a crew.
  • Optionality is intact. Staying solo for 3 years doesn't preclude hiring in year 4 — but hiring in year 1 closes most of the other doors.

The "minor player" reframing

"Minor player even within Walterboro" is what the main analysis said — and it's only true if you measure by reviews and visibility. By the metric that actually pays the mortgage — profit per hour worked — a low-overhead solo at $130–160/hr on jobs you chose is winning a different game than a fully-staffed yard at $110/hr with $40k/mo of fixed costs. Don't confuse the leaderboards.

The strategic claim

Walterboro is not the consolation prize for someone who couldn't make it on the coast. It's a structurally cheaper place to run a high-margin niche. The internet collapses the geography that used to matter — for decking kits, console panels, and surveyor-referred glass work, your address is a postage label. The only thing the coast still has that Walterboro doesn't is dockside service, and you've already conceded that segment.

The Compound Effect — Just Cut & Price, 3 and 5 Years Out

No hire, no Beaufort, no productized kits yet — just the three free moves: raise rates, drop the bottom 20% of jobs, tighten process. What does that alone do?

The cut: which 20% goes

Jobs to drop cut

  • Driveway / patio soda-blasting (not boats — distracts the brand)
  • $200 wash-and-wax requests
  • Trailer-only repair jobs (low ticket, high hassle)
  • "Diagnose my mystery engine problem" jobs you can't quote
  • Out-of-town one-offs that don't anchor a repeat customer
  • Anyone who's haggled twice before agreeing to a number

~20% of jobs, but only ~8–10% of revenue — and disproportionately the burnout drivers.

Jobs to keep & double down on keep

  • EVA marine decking (signature, visual, high-margin)
  • Pontoon floor replacements
  • Fiberglass & gelcoat restoration (full jobs, not patches)
  • CNC console / dash panels (already productized)
  • RV decking & foam (broadens base, off-season smoother)
  • Insurance / surveyor-referred jobs (paid by check, never haggle)

Schedule Scenarios — 8 to 5, No Exceptions

Two specific work-week shapes you asked about, modeled side-by-side. Both lock in 8 AM start, 5 PM stop, hour for lunch = 8 productive hours/day. Both apply the disciplined-pricing path from the previous tab. Both stay solo in Walterboro.

A · Snowbird Mode

5 days/wk8–52 months off (Dec–Jan)

Mon–Fri, 40 hrs/wk × 44 work weeks = 1,760 hrs/yr in the shop. Take December and January off entirely — the slow season on the water is also the cold season in the shop. Use the break for travel, family, or the snowbird teaching gig (see Moves).

B · Four-Day Week

4 days/wk8–52-wk vacation

Mon–Thu, 32 hrs/wk × 50 work weeks = 1,600 hrs/yr in the shop. Every weekend is a 3-day weekend. Friday is for admin, errands, kid stuff, or just rest. One conventional 2-week summer vacation.

5-year revenue side-by-side · Scenario A vs. B

Same rate/productization path applied to both. Scenario A wins on cumulative revenue (~$100k more over 5 years) because it has 160 more shop-hours per year. Scenario B wins on weekly recovery and is more sustainable if the body can't handle Mon–Fri at 50.

The honest trade-offs

Snowbird Mode — why it wins (and where it bites)

  • +$100k cumulative over 5 years vs. four-day.
  • Two months of real rest is rarer than 52 long weekends — and it's the time-block big enough to travel, take a class, build a new product line, or run paid workshops.
  • Customers learn the rhythm: "off Dec–Jan, book by Nov." Forces a waitlist and seasonal pricing.
  • Bite: 10 straight months without a real break can still grind you down. The 3-day weekend in Scenario B has compounding recovery value the snowbird block doesn't.
  • Bite: two months of zero cash flow needs reserves — at least 3 months expenses in the bank before this is safe.

Four-Day Week — why it wins (and where it bites)

  • Every weekend is 3 days. The single highest-impact recovery upgrade in the trades.
  • Steady year-round cash flow — no winter hole to plan around.
  • Easier to staff a 1099 prep helper into (see Moves) because the schedule is predictable.
  • Friday off = errands and admin off the weekend, so weekends are genuinely yours.
  • Bite: ~$100k less over 5 years.
  • Bite: no big time-block for new ventures, workshops, or a real trip.

Recommendation: start B, earn A

Year 1 is the burnout-recovery year. Start with Scenario B (4-day week) — every weekend long, all year. Once the waitlist is established and the books are clean (~year 2), graduate to Scenario A (Snowbird) if the winter block becomes more valuable than the weekly rhythm — most likely if you want to teach SeaDek workshops, build out the mail-order kit business, or just travel. They're not exclusive — they're sequential.

Assumption discipline: both models assume 70% billable utilization (admin, ordering, cleanup, no-shows eat the rest), parts/materials at ~30% of labor revenue, and a productized side that grows from $0 in year 1 to ~$100k in year 5 (decking kits + CNC panels). Drop productization to zero and Scenario A still does ~$1.05M / 5 yrs; ~$0.95M for Scenario B. Both still beat status quo.

Moves the Main Plan Didn't List

A dozen levers that fit inside the "stay solo, stay in Walterboro" frame. Ranked by readiness — green = do this in 90 days, teal = year 1–2, purple = optional / opportunistic.

1Trailer-mounted mobile rig · Beaufort upside without the lease

Build a self-contained mobile rig (generator, compressor, blast media, small CNC for on-site templating). Drive to Beaufort or HHI 2–4 days/month, charge coastal rates ($130–145/hr), sleep at home that night. Captures most of the Beaufort upside without rent, harbormaster politics, or a crew. Pre-book through marina dockmasters as referrals.

Capex ~$8–15k · payback <6 months · zero overhead drag
21099 prep helper · stay technically solo, double capacity

Hire a high-school grad or trade-school student as a 1099 IC on prep work only — sanding, blasting, masking, hauling. SubKit-style state-compliant agreement (CA AB5 doesn't apply in SC; SC's IC test is friendlier). You stay solo legally, but your skilled hours go entirely to finish work. Easy to scale up/down with demand.

~$20–25/hr cost · adds 30–40% capacity · no W-2 / no benefits / no employer tax
3Surveyor & insurance rolodex · the Bob's Dry Dock model

Bob's Dry Dock in Beaufort gets cited as "trusted by surveyors and insurers." That's a quality signal worth more than any ad. Spend a quarter getting on the rolodex of 3–5 marine surveyors and 2 insurance adjusters in the corridor. They send glass & structural jobs, the bill goes to a check-cutter, and you never haggle.

Cost: lunches + reliability · payoff: highest-trust, lowest-effort lead source in the trade
4Mail-order EVA decking kits · the CNC working while you sleep

Digitize templates for the top 15 pontoon and center-console models. Cut once, ship USPS to DIY installers nationwide at $300–500/kit ($150–250 margin). The CNC machine that's idle 60% of the time becomes a passive product line. Ships in a flat-rate box. Demand is national; your competitive set is SeaDek's national network — you compete on price + model selection.

Year 1 target: 50 kits · Year 5: 400+ kits · no install labor, no scheduling
5Subcontract to other yards · their overhead, your craft

Be the EVA / fiberglass sub for Charleston and Beaufort yards. They handle the customer, the slip, the diagnosis, the billing — you do the work that's your craft. Bill $145/hr to the yard (they bill the customer $185). No marketing required, captive demand, and you ride the waterfront brand without paying for it.

Best fit: Marsh Harbor (Beaufort), Hanckel (Charleston), Matthews (Bluffton)
6Pre-paid annual maintenance plans · recurring revenue

Sell a "Decking + Detailing Care Plan" — annual prepay, $1,200–2,400, covers a yearly detail, one decking touch-up, priority booking. Locks in revenue, smooths winter cash flow, and the recurring customer is who you actually want — repeat owner, knows the work, recommends you.

Target: 20–40 plans by Yr 3 = $30–80k baseline revenue
7Dry storage on owned land · passive revenue

If you have land, fence a corner, dust gravel, install a gate camera, and rent dry storage at $3–4/ft/month. 20 boats × 25 ft × $3.50 × 12 = ~$21k/yr for ~5 hours/month of work. Pairs naturally with service — storage customers become service customers and vice versa.

Capex: ~$5–10k (gravel + fence + camera) · 1–2 yr payback
8Snowbird teaching · only if Scenario A

During the Dec–Jan break in Scenario A, host weekend SeaDek installer certification workshops at the shop. 8 students × $1,000/wknd × 4 wknds = $32k off-season. Doubles as content marketing (every graduate becomes a quiet referrer) and validates you as the Lowcountry decking authority.

Requires SeaDek dealer cert · only viable in 5-day Snowbird schedule
9CNC rental / job-shop nights · idle-asset monetization

The CNC machine sits idle ~60% of the time. Open evening / weekend slots to local hobbyists, wood shops, sign-makers at $50/hr unattended (you set the job up, they collect the parts). Or take small CNC jobs from non-boat customers. Low-friction add-on, no employee headache.

~$5–15k/yr passive · doesn't scale, but doesn't cost anything either
10Productized fixed-price packages · phone screen for tire-kickers

3–4 packaged decking/restoration tiers on the website with fixed prices and clear photos. Tire-kickers self-screen ("not for me"), serious buyers self-qualify ("the $4,800 one — let's book"). Eliminates the "can you give me a quote" call entirely. This is the cheapest pricing-power upgrade you can make.

Build cost: a weekend · revenue effect: ongoing margin lift
11Open-source the dash-panel configurator · lead-gen flywheel

A web tool where boat owners design their own dash panel (pick console model, lay out gauges/switches, see a preview). Free to design, $400+ to ship. SEO ranks for "custom boat dash panel" indefinitely. Becomes the entry point for a national CNC-panel business that runs while you sleep.

Ambitious · needs ~$8–15k of dev + a designer · highest upside lever
12License the EVA decking templates · royalties from other shops

Once you've digitized the top 30 pontoon and console templates, license them to other small shops for $200/pattern + 5% royalty on each install. You stop being the only shop in America that can cut a perfect 2020 Bennington floor; instead, you're the IP holder. Long game, but the asset compounds.

Most ambitious · year 3+ only · turns a service business into IP

The unifying thread

Every one of these moves either monetizes an asset you already own (CNC, land, templates, reputation), charges coastal rates without coastal overhead (mobile rig, subcontracting, mail-order), or buys recurring revenue without a hire (maintenance plans, storage, surveyor rolodex). None of them require Beaufort. Most of them require nothing but a focused quarter.
