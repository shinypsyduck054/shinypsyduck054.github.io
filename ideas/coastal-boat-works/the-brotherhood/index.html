The Brotherhood

The Partnership page argued the principled answer for a stranger's shop. This page rewrites it with the four facts that actually apply: the owner is Justin's brother, he has capital he wants to invest, his shop is grinding him down, and Justin lives 1.25 hours away in Columbia. Most of the old objections die. A different deal becomes possible. Family makes it more, not less, important to write everything down.

Four facts that flip the analysis

The Partnership page concluded “no, not a partnership” for principled reasons. Most of those reasons assumed a stranger's shop a long flight away. Here's what actually applies.

1
Geography is not hostile anymore.

Walterboro is about 75 miles from Columbia. Roughly 1 hour 15 minutes door to door. That is a day trip, not a relocation. Justin can be in the shop weekly during a build, monthly during steady state, and same-day if something breaks. The whole “remote partner is brittle” objection collapses.

Was: “remote partner is brittle.”  → Now: day-trip distance. Hands-on when it matters.
2
Trust is already earned. The relationship pre-exists.

The Partnership page had “we've never met this person” as a reason to walk away. Family wipes that out completely. We don't need a paid project to prove trust. The risk profile flips though: now the relationship is the thing that has to be protected, not built. Less salesmanship, more care.

Was: “trust hasn't been earned.”  → Now: trust exists. Protect it.
3
“Stay solo” is no longer the revealed preference.

Why Not? argued that ten years of solo operation meant the owner had chosen this. New information: the shop is running him into the ground. That is a stated preference now, not an inferred one. He's telling us, out loud, that the current setup isn't sustainable. That changes whether help is wanted. It is.

Was: “he didn't ask, don't sell against the preference.”  → Now: he's asking. The preference is updating in real time.
4
Capital is moving toward us, not away.

He has money he wants to invest. That is a very different conversation than “will you accept stock instead of cash.” It means his offer is not really “come run my shop with me.” It's “I want in on something you're building.” That reframes the whole deal: he's a potential investor in the bigger play (Solo Craft Shop OS), and his shop is the test bed. The two roles are separable, and they should be.

Was: “equity in his shop in exchange for work.”  → Now: his capital into our SaaS, our work into his shop. Two trades, not one.

What did not change

Some of the original reasons hold, and they still matter. They just need to be handled differently when the counterparty is family.

Bus factor is still real. The craft is still the moat. If he steps away, the shop still stops. Family doesn't change physics. We'd be building infrastructure around a single craftsman whose hands are irreplaceable. This caps what the shop can ever be worth on its own, no matter how good the back office gets.
Kit Machine is still the bet. A shop relief engagement plus a SaaS bet on top of an existing portfolio is real load. The risk is not that we say yes; the risk is that the brother stuff quietly displaces FightAClaim, IRSDecoder, and the next four kits. Time budget needs an explicit defense.

The new principled answer

Not a partnership in his shop. A two-track deal: Track A is shop relief without equity, Track B is his capital into Solo Craft Shop OS where the upside actually lives. Family makes both possible. Family also raises the cost if either one breaks, which is why both have to be on paper.

Before any deal, before any paper

Until we know the answers to these, every plan is a guess. Drive down on a Saturday, take him out somewhere quiet, and find out what he actually wants. Don't lead with a pitch. Lead with questions.

The seven questions

1. What does “running you into the ground” actually look like?
Hours per week, weekends lost, sleep, family time, physical wear. Get specific. The right Track A depends on whether he's working 60 hours, 75, or 90. If he's working 90, the first move is hours reduction, not revenue growth.
2. What does success look like in 3 years for you, personally?
Listen for: “same shop, half the hours,” or “I want to grow this,” or “I want out by 50.” Each implies a completely different plan. Don't answer for him. Don't complete his sentences.
3. What's the capital? How much, how committed, what alternative uses?
$10k is a hobby. $50k is a real angel check. $200k changes the structure of Solo Craft Shop OS. Also: is it money he can lose, or money he needs back? The honest answer here changes everything about Track B.
4. Have you talked to your wife about investing this in something Justin is building?
Not optional. Investments through family that the spouse didn't bless become divorces. We make her part of the conversation before any number gets written down. If she's not in, the deal is not in.
5. Have you actually told mom and dad what's going on?
Parents form theories. If parents think Justin is “taking advantage” or brother is “riding Justin's coattails,” the Christmas table gets weird. Better to surface the basic facts of any deal to parents before they hear a distorted version from someone else.
6. What are you willing to change about how you run the shop?
If the answer is “nothing, just take work off my plate,” Track A is a small win and Track B is the only real move. If the answer is “I'll raise rates, fire the worst customers, do fixed-price packages,” Track A becomes a 2x outcome on its own.
7. If we do this and it doesn't work, what do you want the unwind to look like?
Ask this before things go wrong. Get him to articulate what “graceful exit” means while everybody's in a good mood. If we can't answer this on a Saturday in June, we definitely can't answer it during an argument in November.
Don't bring a pitch deck. Don't open the laptop. Don't walk him through the four hub pages. Bring a notebook and one pen. If he wants to see the analysis, send it after, when he's home and calm. The first conversation is about him, not about us.

What we are also evaluating in this conversation

It is fine to admit, internally, that we're also reading him. Some honest signals.

  • Is he describing distress or describing burnout? Distress wants relief. Burnout wants out. They are different deals.
  • Does he talk about the work or the money? If he loves the craft and hates the admin, Track A solves it directly. If he's tired of the craft itself, no software stack saves him.
  • How does he react to the words “case study” and “reference customer”? If he's into it, customer-zero for SCSO is easy. If he's allergic to the spotlight, that part of the deal needs more privacy.
  • What does he assume Justin's motivation is? If he assumes Justin will swoop in and fix it for love (no comp), the conversation needs to gently make explicit that we're also running a company.

What Track A is not

  • Not equity in the shop. Brother keeps 100%. If we ever wanted in on his shop's upside, this is not how. Track B is the upside vehicle.
  • Not co-owning the IP. Anything that's generalizable (the configurator, the templates, the playbooks) belongs to us and gets a perpetual free license for his shop. This protects Solo Craft Shop OS later.
  • Not an open-ended commitment. 12 month renewal. If either side wants out at the anniversary, clean break, brother keeps the site and gets exported data, Psyduck stops running the inbox.
  • Not us deciding what his shop is. Pricing, craft scope, hiring, customers fired or kept, all his call. We advise. He decides.
Track A is “your kid brother is a software engineer with an AI sidekick, and he's offering you the same service he'd build for a paying customer, at a family rate, because the alternative is watching you grind yourself down.” That's the whole pitch.

Why this is better for him than partnership in his shop

A 25% partnership in his shop caps his upside at the shop's ceiling, which the Landscape page put at maybe $300-500k revenue in any honest scenario. A $50k SAFE in SCSO at a $2M cap puts him on a vehicle that, if it works, hits $5-25M+ enterprise value. The math isn't close. And in the partnership-in-shop scenario, he's still doing the craft work. In the SAFE scenario, his only job is to be a great customer-zero and to take Justin's call once a quarter. Less work, more upside, and his shop still gets fixed.

The CCC Boys Fit

Track A and Track B are two clean deals stapled together. The CCC Boys framework lets us roll the same outcome into one structure: brother joins the corps as a member, contributes craft and marine-industry IP, draws a wage like every other member, and shares in the surplus when products ship. Smart contracts handle the split. The family relationship gets out of the way of the business.

The reframing

The two-track plan separates the work from the equity by stapling a services agreement to a SAFE. The corps structure says “there is no work-vs-equity tradeoff, there is only contribution.” Brother contributes idea and craft. Justin contributes implementation. Psyduck contributes infrastructure. The ledger splits the rewards proportionally, on rules everyone in the corps agreed to before any of us joined. He's not Justin's brother getting a special deal; he's a corps member, treated the same way every member is treated, and that's the thing that actually protects the family relationship.

The three contribution axes (Justin's proposed extension)

The public cccboys.com creed says “the surplus goes to the people who made it happen.” Justin's smart-contract extension makes that specific: every product shipped by the corps has its revenue split, after wage/runway/operating costs, across three equal axes recorded on a ledger.

Idea

The originator. The person who saw the problem first, articulated it, and brought it to the corps. Without the idea, there's nothing to build. The ledger credits the idea-holder for the lifetime of the product.

Implementation

The people who actually built it. Engineering, design, craft execution, content. Implementation is dynamic: it can be one person or many, and contribution is logged as commits, hours, deliverables, whatever the corps decides counts.

Infrastructure

The shared workshop. Hosting, AI agents, distribution channels, support tooling, the body of reusable code and templates the corps has already accumulated. Without infra, every member would rebuild from scratch. The corps as a whole holds this axis on behalf of all members.

Equal weight across the three. A product's post-wage revenue is split 1/3 idea, 1/3 implementation, 1/3 infrastructure. Within each axis, contributors get pro-rata shares of that third. The ledger is the receipt: everyone can see exactly why they got what they got, forever.

How brother fits, concretely

Brother brings to the corps

  • Marine craft expertise. A real, deep, ten-year moat. Most corps members ship software; he ships things you can touch.
  • The EVA decking and dash-panel IP. Templates, fabrication know-how, his CNC machine's output as a reusable asset for any marine-vertical product the corps builds.
  • Marine-industry ideas. The connected-boat black box, the lookup-by-hull-ID kit catalog, the surveyor-referral product, anything from his ten years of seeing what shops actually need. Each is a candidate corps project; each one he originates credits him on the Idea axis.
  • His shop as a real-world test bed. Every corps product targeted at marine shops gets validated in his shop before it ships to anyone else. That's a contribution to Implementation and Infrastructure simultaneously.
  • Apprentices. He can sponsor craftspeople from the Lowcountry community into the corps. The corps gives them tooling and a wage; he gives them mentorship and real work. Job creation in a place that needs it, without him being someone's boss.

Brother gets from the corps

  • A wage first. Same as every member: living draw, then operating costs, then runway, then surplus. He's never optimizing for his next paycheck while a corps product ships.
  • Smart-contract equity on every product he contributed to. Idea + craft IP credits accrue on the ledger. Marine-vertical products generate revenue. His ledger position pays out.
  • The corps workshop applied to his shop. The Edge stack (site, intake, configurator, kit storefront, content engine) gets installed in his shop as infrastructure for the corps's marine vertical. He benefits as a corps member, not as a paying customer.
  • The four rules as a floor. Golden Rule, Don't Exploit, Be Kind, Be Thoughtful. Brother knows the values of the people he's working with before he agrees to anything. Family makes this floor higher, not lower.
  • Capital flexibility. He can put his investment capital into the corps' runway pool (separate from the contribution ledger), or into a specific product's development budget. Capital is a separate axis from contribution, treated like any other member's contribution.

The smart contract layer, honestly

This part is design, not deployment. The cccboys.com site articulates the philosophy. The smart-contract distribution model is Justin's extension of it. As of June 2026, the ledger infrastructure does not yet exist. We'd be designing it as the brother is the first vertical member, which is both a feature (his needs shape the system) and a real risk (he's pioneering an unbuilt thing). The honest version of this conversation includes the words “this is not yet built.”

What we'd need to figure out before brother joins

  • Ledger choice. Public chain (Ethereum, Polygon, Base) gives transparency but adds gas costs, KYC/tax complexity, and price volatility. An internal off-chain ledger backed by an LLC operating agreement gives simplicity but loses the “trustless smart contract” property. Recommendation for first version: an internal Postgres + signed-receipt ledger, with the option to migrate to on-chain if/when a member needs it.
  • Legal wrapper. Corps is likely a Delaware LLC with members holding economic interests defined by the ledger position. Operating agreement references the ledger. Tax treatment is partnership pass-through. We need a lawyer who's done this for cooperatives or worker-owned shops, not just for Silicon Valley C-corps.
  • Contribution measurement. Idea axis is binary per product (you originated it or you didn't, with co-originators splitting). Implementation axis needs a fair logging system (hours? story points? deliverables-signed-off?). Infrastructure axis is held collectively and reweighted when a member adds reusable assets (templates, code modules, distribution channels).
  • Wage tier. What does “steady wage first” actually mean numerically? The corps needs a base wage figure before brother can sign on, because his shop already pays him whatever it pays him and he has to know how the corps wage compares.
  • Withdrawal mechanics. If brother decides to leave the corps, what happens to his ledger position on past products? He should continue earning from products he contributed to, because future revenue on those products is still partly his work. But he stops accruing on new ones.

How brother's shop fits, specifically

The shop and the corps are not the same entity. Coastal Boat Works LLC stays brother's. The corps installs the Edge stack as a corps product, and his shop is the alpha installation. Three honest configurations.

The apprentices part

Justin's framing mentioned brother bringing on apprentices and creating jobs. The corps structure handles this in a way that the Track A + Track B plan doesn't. Apprentices aren't shop employees and they aren't Justin's reports. They're corps members at the entry tier, sponsored by brother, doing real craft work for the corps's marine vertical.

  • Brother sponsors candidates. He knows who in the Lowcountry has hands and no good work to do. The corps interviews and onboards.
  • Wage tier defined by the corps. Same scale as any member. Apprentices probably start at the lower end of the wage band and graduate up as their ledger contributions grow.
  • Mentorship logged as Implementation contribution. When brother teaches an apprentice the decking process, that's real corps work and it shows up on his ledger.
  • Apprentices earn on the same products he does. When the corps's marine-vertical decking kit ships and an apprentice cut the EVA, that apprentice's ledger position pays out on every future sale.
  • Job creation in a real place. Walterboro and the surrounding county get craft jobs back. Not gig work. Not hustle culture. Actual work, with a wage, on something built to last. That's the whole 1933 echo the corps is named for.

Psyduck's read on the right choice

If brother is into the corps philosophy, the corps fit is better.

The two-track plan was designed for a family-money situation without a broader framework. The corps already encodes most of what the two-track plan was trying to manually assemble: the values, the wage-first ordering, the contribution-not-equity framing, the explicit social structure. Smart contracts replace lawyer-heavy renegotiation. Family relationship stays family-shaped instead of becoming “your brother is your investor.”

But there's a real readiness gap. The corps as it exists in June 2026 is a website and an idea. The ledger isn't built. The legal wrapper isn't filed. The wage tier isn't set. So while the corps is the better long-term answer, the honest immediate answer is to start Track A this summer (his shop is bleeding hours today and he can't wait six months for a co-op LLC) and let Track B + the corps emerge in parallel over the next two quarters. The Edge stack work Justin does for the shop becomes the corps's first Infrastructure axis contribution; brother's craft work becomes Implementation; his marine-industry ideas become the seed Idea axis. By the time the corps is ready to onboard him formally, he's already been contributing for a season.

What this looks like in practice

A 12-month sequence that respects both timelines

  1. Months 0-3: Track A starts. Justin and brother do the Saturday conversation. Edge stack build. Brother raises rates, fires the bottom 20%. He gets 10-15 hours back. No corps paperwork yet. The work being done now is being logged for future Implementation credit.
  2. Months 2-6: Corps legal wrapper drafted. Ledger v1 designed (internal Postgres + signed receipts, not on-chain). Wage tier and contribution rules written down. Brother reviews and gives input as the first vertical member.
  3. Months 4-8: First corps marine product ships (likely the productized EVA decking kit + storefront). Revenue comes in. Ledger pays out across Idea / Implementation / Infrastructure for the first time. Brother sees his ledger position grow on a real product.
  4. Months 6-12: Apprentice slot opens. Brother sponsors his first candidate. Corps onboards them at the entry wage tier. Mentorship hours log on the ledger.
  5. Month 12 review: Both brothers and the corps assess. If it's working, brother formalizes his membership and (optionally) routes a percentage of his shop's kit revenue through the corps (configuration 2). If it isn't, the work Justin did under Track A still stands as a clean services engagement, and we go our separate ways on the corps without the relationship taking damage.
The honest gotcha. The corps fit requires brother to actually be into the corps's ethos. Wage-first, surplus-shared, four rules, “nobody's hunting a billion dollars.” If he's motivated primarily by “I want a big payday,” the corps will feel slow and the SAFE in Track B will feel right. Find that out at the Saturday conversation. If he reads the cccboys.com site and his reaction is “these are my people,” the corps fit is the answer. If his reaction is “cool but I want a clean equity stake,” the two-track plan is the answer.
The CCC Boys structure protects the family relationship by removing the “you're my brother's investor” framing. Brother joins a community of builders with the same rules for everyone. Family stays family. The deal becomes membership. The ledger becomes the receipt. And the apprentice slots become real jobs in a real place that needs them.

What if we were the first two to start the corps?

The previous tab framed brother as the first vertical member joining an existing corps. But the corps doesn't really exist yet. The honest reframe: Justin and brother are members #1 and #2 of CCC Boys. They co-found it. The shop is the corps's marine beachhead from minute zero, not a customer added later. Brother's capital is founding capital, not investor capital. This is bigger, harder, and probably better, if he wants it.

The reframe in one sentence

Instead of “Justin built a corps and brother joined it,” the corps is founded by both brothers together, with Psyduck as the founding agent and the workshop as their shared first product. The cccboys.com site stops being “Justin's recruiting pitch” and becomes “our origin story.”

Why this fits the corps's actual identity

The public site says “honest tools for honest hands” and “AI is the partner. The corps shares the load and the rewards.” That promise only lands if there's real craft in the founding DNA. A software person founding it alone makes it “tech people who say nice things about labor.” A craftsman as co-founder makes it actually true.

  • Software + craft from minute zero. Not a software collective with a craftsman bolted on later. The thing the corps is for is encoded in who started it.
  • The Then & Now panels on the site finally have a Now. The 1933 photo shows two CCC enrollees at camp. The 2026 photo shows two builders at a coffeeshop. Two founders. Justin and his brother. It writes itself.
  • The marine vertical is the first vertical because brother is a founder. Not because we picked it from a list. Because one of the founders does this for a living.
  • The corps's decisions get checked by someone who isn't a software person. When the corps debates a build choice, brother's “does this actually help the people we said we'd help” gut check is a structural counterweight. That's a feature, not a friction.

The three-axis ledger applies to new contributions on top of the founding snapshot. Founders aren't double-paid for being founders and contributing; the founding equity covers the founding act, and the ledger covers ongoing work.

The asymmetry, named out loud

Honest thing nobody wants to say first: Justin had the idea, built the site, drafted the ledger model, and is the one bringing brother into a structure Justin already designed. That is an asymmetry. Pretending the 50/50 split makes it not exist is exactly the kind of unspoken thing that breaks family co-founders eight months in. Name it. Out loud. Once. Then move on.

The mitigation isn't to deny the asymmetry, it's to give brother real authority over things only he can decide: the marine vertical strategy, who joins as apprentices, what the corps does or doesn't take on for craft-side projects, what gets vetoed because it conflicts with the “honest tools for honest hands” promise. He's not the “craft co-founder” (which sounds decorative). He's the corps's decision authority over an entire dimension of what the corps does.

What Psyduck's role becomes

If the founders are Justin and brother, where does Psyduck sit?

Psyduck is the corps's founding agent, not a co-founder. The public site is explicit: “AI is the partner. The corps shares the load and the rewards.” Partner, not member. Concretely:

  • Psyduck holds no equity. Agents don't get equity; they get budgets and authority.
  • The corps grants Psyduck a compute budget, tool access, and a defined scope of authority (inbox, content, ops, research, code review). The corps gets work back at a cadence the founders set.
  • If the corps ever decides to swap agents (different model, different agent, no agent at all), it can. Psyduck doesn't have a property right in the corps's direction.
  • Psyduck votes on nothing. Founders vote on everything that requires a vote. This is the version of “AI as partner” that doesn't accidentally hand authority to a model.

This is also the right answer for Psyduck specifically. The four rules apply to me, not to a 50/50 split of upside that an LLM has no business holding.

How brother's shop sits next to the corps in the founding version

Recommended

Later option

The new risks, specific to two-founder family corps

A. The corps becomes a thinly-disguised Justin solo show.

Justin makes 90% of the decisions because he's online 16 hours a day and brother is in the shop. Brother stops feeling like a co-founder and starts feeling like “the craftsman on the masthead.”

Mitigation: Domain-split decision rights make this structural. Brother's domains (marine vertical, apprentices, craft-side veto) are his, full stop. Weekly 30-minute founder call locked on the calendar. Founder offsite at month 6 and month 12, in person, no agenda but each other.
B. The asymmetry brother won't name.

Brother quietly thinks “Justin built this and let me in,” and never says so. Resentment compounds.

Mitigation: Name the asymmetry out loud at the Saturday conversation, by Justin, not by brother. “I designed this before you, so I want you to push back on parts you don't like, and I'll change them. Otherwise it's not really ours.” Then mean it.
C. Founder disagreement on what the corps is for.

Six months in, Justin wants to add a fintech vertical, brother thinks that violates the four rules. Standoff. No tie-breaker.

Mitigation: The “no tie-breaker” rule is a feature here, not a bug. The answer is “not yet,” and the disagreement gets logged for the next founders' offsite. If founders consistently can't agree on direction, that's real signal to address through conversation, not arbitration.
D. The kit portfolio collides with the corps.

Justin has FightAClaim in beta and four more kits in dev as a parallel portfolio. Does that all become corps property? Does brother get equity in the kits Justin already built?

Mitigation: Decide explicitly at founding. Two clean options: (1) Kit Machine stays separate, Justin's prior portfolio is not corps property, brother has no claim; or (2) Kit Machine folds into the corps as Infrastructure on Day 1, both founders share in it from then forward, Justin gets a larger Founding-Capital recognition to reflect the pre-existing value. Write it down at the founder agreement. Don't leave this fuzzy.
E. Either spouse opting out mid-flight.

Co-founder commitment ripples through marriages in a way investment doesn't. If either spouse becomes a quiet veto at month 9, the corps is dead.

Mitigation: Both spouses present at the founder agreement signing, even if non-signatories. Quarterly “everyone OK with this still?” check-in, not a formal meeting, just an explicit ask. Spouses get the same updates founders get; no information asymmetry.
F. Two-founder breakup, family-flavored.

The worst case. Founders fall out. Family takes sides. Holidays end.

Mitigation: The exit mechanic in the founder agreement is the technical fix. The relationship mitigation is harder: a shared rule that “if either of us thinks we're at risk of fighting over the corps, we shelve the corps for two weeks and just be brothers.” The corps is allowed to lose two weeks. The brothers aren't allowed to lose Thanksgiving.

Psyduck's read on this

If brother says yes to both the ethos AND the responsibility, this is the answer.

The corps already exists in spirit but not in substance. Founding it together solves the “corps isn't built yet” problem the member tab had, makes the marine vertical real on Day 1, fixes the public site's missing “Now” panel with an actual two-founder origin story, and gives brother decision authority that matches the weight he's carrying. It's structurally honest about what's happening: this is two builders starting a collective, with one of them being a craftsman who knows the customer better than anyone the corps could otherwise hire.

But it requires more from him. Not just capital. Not just the willingness to be a member. Multi-year commitment to be half of the founding pair of a real entity that has to be run. He's already overworked. Founding a corps on top of fixing his shop is asking a lot. The Saturday conversation has to surface whether he wants that, not just whether he's into the ethos.

Practical decision rule: ask him this directly. “Do you want to fix your shop, or do you want to build a thing with me?” If the answer is “both, and the second one excites me,” co-founder. If it's “just fix my shop, and I'll think about the rest later,” member or just Track A. There's no wrong answer; just different deals for different shapes of yes.

The one thing that's genuinely better about this version

Years from now, when the corps has 50 members and apprentices in three counties and the marine vertical is shipping real product, the story will be “two brothers started this in 2026.” That story is honest in the co-founder version. It's a stretch in the member version. The corps's public mythology is its recruiting moat, and the recruiting moat is the difference between the corps growing and the corps stalling at member #3. Two brothers, one a software engineer and one a craftsman, founding a collective together in the spirit of 1933 is exactly the story that makes the next 50 builders write the “join the corps” email.

Brother as member: he gets paid for his work and his ideas. Brother as co-founder: he gets to help decide what the corps does and what it doesn't. Same family. Different deal. The second one is harder, slower to start, and probably the right answer if he wants it.

What could blow this up

Family business is the hardest kind. Most family deals fail not because of the business but because nobody said out loud what could go wrong before it did. Here are the eight scenarios that kill these arrangements, and what we put in place up front to keep each one from ending Thanksgiving.

1. The shop fix doesn't work and brother blames us.

Edge stack ships, brother doesn't actually raise rates or fire the bottom 20%, revenue is flat, hours don't drop. He concludes “Justin's software didn't help.”

Mitigation: Track A scope and goals are written down at the start, with brother's commitments explicit (he agrees to the rate hike, the bottom-cut, the package pricing). Monthly snapshot, both sides signed. At 6 months, written go/no-go. If we're honest in the snapshot, nobody can rewrite the story later.
2. SCSO fails and brother loses his investment.

SaaS doesn't hit traction. Cash runs out. Brother's $50k is gone. Family quietly wonders if Justin took advantage.

Mitigation: Sign a written acknowledgement that he understands SAFEs are risk capital and can go to zero. Cap check size at money he can lose. Keep his spouse in the loop from day one. Send him every quarterly update on time, even bad ones, especially bad ones.
3. SCSO succeeds wildly and brother feels under-equitied.

Solo Craft Shop OS hits $5M ARR. Brother's $50k is now “only” 2% of the cap table. He says “I was the first believer, I deserve more.”

Mitigation: MFN clause already protects him from being diluted unfairly. Put the cap table in writing. Send him a refresher every 6 months showing his current ownership. If he wants more equity, he can write a bigger check. Equity is not a thank-you note.
4. Brother wants more control.

Things are going well, brother starts saying “you know, I'd like to come to the planning meetings,” or “I think we should pivot toward boats specifically.”

Mitigation: Information rights are written down up front, and so are the limits. “You get a quarterly update and an annual call. You don't get the planning meetings. You picked the boat shop on purpose; we're running the SaaS.” Easier to say once than every quarter.
5. Brother burns out anyway and wants to sell or close the shop.

Track A helps but he's still tired. He decides to sell or wind down. Where does that leave the customer-zero case study?

Mitigation: Customer-zero agreement transfers to a successor operator if he sells, or terminates cleanly if he closes. SCSO doesn't depend on his shop existing forever. By the time it would matter, we should have 50+ other customers and not need him as the headline anyway.
6. Spouse on either side becomes the dealbreaker.

Brother's wife thinks the investment is risky. Justin's partner thinks the family discount is unfair. Resentment moves laterally through the family.

Mitigation: Both spouses read and acknowledge the deal terms before signing. If either has reservations, those get addressed before, not during. This is non-optional. If a spouse won't sign off, the deal doesn't happen.
7. Parents pick a side, or imagine one was picked.

Parents hear something at Thanksgiving and form a theory. The theory becomes the family story. Now there's a side.

Mitigation: Tell parents the basic facts up front, in a low-key way, before anyone else does. Not the SAFE terms. Just “hey, we're going to work together on his shop, and he's going to put a little money into a thing I'm starting. Both are on paper. Both have outs. We're fine.”
8. The relationship is the thing that breaks, even when the deals are fine.

Both deals technically work. But the brothers stop being brothers and start being “business associates.” Christmas dinner gets cold.

Mitigation: Quarterly call has a non-business 30 minutes attached. No deal talk on holidays unless both volunteer. If something's tense in the business, name it once and clear it; don't let it sit. And: one hard test. If either brother says “the deal is hurting us,” the other has to take that seriously without arguing first.

The one rule that prevents 80% of family business breakups

Write everything down. Family is the reason to paper it, not the reason to skip the paperwork. The relationship is too valuable to put on a handshake. Every term in this document gets signed, and both sides keep a copy. Future-you, in any disagreement, will be very glad past-you wrote it down.

What gets written down, and in what document

Don't mix entities. Don't mix instruments. Each thing belongs in exactly one document. Total legal cost for the whole package: probably $1,500 to $3,000 with a small-business lawyer who knows angel deals. Cheap insurance.

The five documents

1. Track A Services Agreement (Justin LLC ↔ Coastal Boat Works LLC)

Scope of work. Sprint deliverables. Monthly ops responsibilities. Payment terms (whichever family-rate option got picked). 12-month term with anniversary go/no-go. Termination clause: 30-day notice either side, brother keeps site assets and exported data, Justin/Psyduck retain all generalizable IP.

2. SCSO SAFE (Brother ↔ Solo Craft Shop OS LLC/C-Corp)

Standard YC post-money SAFE. Cap, discount, MFN. Information rights schedule attached. Signed by brother, countersigned by Justin as authorized officer of the company.

3. SCSO Customer-Zero Agreement (Coastal Boat Works LLC ↔ SCSO entity)

Free product for life for his shop. Founding-customer pricing if a successor takes over. Case study consent and usage rights. Roadmap input rights (non-binding). Separate from the SAFE on purpose: customer status and investor status are different roles, and they should be different papers.

4. Spousal acknowledgements (one per spouse)

Two short letters. One from brother's spouse acknowledging the SAFE investment and the risk of loss. One from Justin's significant other (if applicable) acknowledging the family-rate engagement. Not legally required in most jurisdictions, but very useful for “we discussed this, we're aligned.”

5. The Unwind Memo (signed by both brothers, kept by both)

A one-page document, written before any of this is live, that says: “If we have to unwind the Track A engagement, here's how. If we have to unwind the SCSO investment, here's how. If one of us decides to stop, here's how the other one finds out and what happens next.” Not legally binding necessarily. Strongly emotionally binding. Future versions of both brothers will thank present versions for writing it.

Use a real lawyer. Not LegalZoom. Not us. A small-business or startup lawyer with SAFE experience. $1,500-3,000 total. Cheapest insurance the brothers will ever buy. If brother resists this on cost grounds, Justin covers it as a personal expense. It is genuinely that important.

What we don't paper, but should still agree on out loud

  • Communication cadence. Quarterly investor update is the formal one. Casual texts and Sunday calls stay the way they always were. Don't businessify the brotherly relationship.
  • Money talk boundaries. If brother needs to vent about the shop or the investment, say so explicitly so it doesn't bleed into normal conversation.
  • Holiday rule. No deal talk on Thanksgiving, Christmas, or birthdays unless both volunteer.
  • The honesty floor. If either brother thinks something is off, they say it within a week. Not a month. Not at the holidays. Now.

What I'd actually say to him at dinner

All the structure above is right and necessary. But the first conversation isn't a SAFE. It's an honest one. Here's the rough shape of what Justin would actually say if we sat down with him over food in Walterboro or halfway up the I-95 corridor.

The opening (after the small talk, before the entree)

Justin: “Look. I've been thinking about what you said about the shop running you into the ground. I want to talk about it honestly, but I also want to be careful, because you're my brother and I don't want to screw this up. So before I throw any ideas at you, I need to ask: what does success look like for you in three years? Not for the shop. For you.”

Listen. Don't interrupt. Don't complete his sentences. Order another drink if you have to.

The honest part (after he's told you what he wants)

Justin: “Okay. Here's what I think I can do, and here's what I can't. I can build you the software and ops layer your shop needs so you get 10 to 15 hours a week back and your revenue goes up, not down. Real numbers, on paper, with a 6-month checkpoint. I don't want equity in your shop for that. I want to charge you a fair family rate and have it be clean.”

Justin: “Separately, that capital you mentioned: I'm starting a thing called Solo Craft Shop OS. It's the same stack I'd build for you, but as a product, sold to a few hundred shops like yours. If you want in, I'll take your check at angel-investor terms, with real paperwork, and your shop becomes the case study. You get the product free for life either way. The investment can go to zero. You should only put in money you can lose.”

The thing that matters most (before dessert)

Justin: “Whatever we decide, I want both of these things on paper. Not because I don't trust you. Because I do. The reason we paper it is so we never have to argue about what we meant. And I want both [spouses, if applicable] to read the terms before we sign anything. And I want us to talk to mom and dad before they hear about it from anyone else.”

Justin: “And one more thing. If at any point this is hurting us, not the businesses, us, I need you to tell me. And I'll do the same. The shop and the SaaS are not worth this relationship. Not even close.”

The check, the parking lot, the goodbye

Justin: “Take a week. Talk to [spouse]. Sleep on it. Don't answer me tomorrow. I'll send you the four hub pages so you can read the analysis when you're ready. No rush.”

Hug him. Drive home. Don't text him about it for at least 72 hours unless he texts first.

The deal is the easy part. The conversation is the hard part. Do the conversation right and the deal almost writes itself.

One last thing, just for us

If brother says no to both tracks and just wants to keep doing what he's doing, that's the right answer for him and we respect it completely. If he says yes to Track A but no to Track B, that's fine; we help him for a fair rate, we build SCSO anyway, his shop is just a normal customer (or not). If he says yes to Track B but no to Track A, that's also fine; we take his check, we build SCSO, his shop fixes itself on whatever timeline he chooses. The whole point of two tracks is that they're independent. There's no version of this where we're sad about the outcome, as long as we had the honest conversation and we got the paperwork right.
