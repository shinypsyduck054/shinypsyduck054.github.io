Why a roofing vertical inside the corps

This page is downstream of The Magnolia Wildcard, not parallel to it. The Wildcard page asks “is Joey the right cousin to bring in, and in what structure.” This page asks “if yes in any structure beyond Ally, what do we ship together.” If Joey says no to the corps, the roadmap below collapses to whatever subset he'd still pay us to build, which is mostly the Drone Assessment and Roof Claim Kit pieces.

The greenfield argument, said plainly

Joey's Magnolia Roofing in Columbia has 8 years of momentum: established crew, established 1099 culture, established disposal contracts, established brand. None of that exists in the Lowcountry. He hasn't hired a single roofer there yet. He hasn't signed a landfill account. He hasn't chosen a market position.

That means every ethics-by-default decision the corps would want to make about how roofing should be run is cheap in greenfield and expensive in retrofit. Starting a Lowcountry crew as W-2 is a hiring decision. Converting an existing 1099 crew to W-2 is a tax, legal, and payroll-system migration that costs months of operations chaos. Same applies to recycling logistics, brand voice, RE-agent positioning, and crew benefits. The Lowcountry expansion is the one chance to bake the corps's principles into a working business without tearing up something that already exists.

Seven product lines a Lowcountry roofing vertical could ship

Listed roughly in order of capital required to start, lowest first. Each card names the product, the revenue model, the cycle profile, who's contributing what, and whether it clears the corps ethics filters. Some are labor-shaped (the corps employs people who do the work). Some are product-shaped (digital kits, depot infrastructure, drone service) that earn whether or not labor is happening that week.

1. Livable-wage roofing

LABOR · NO NEW CAPITAL
RevenuePer-roof, one-off
CycleHeavily seasonal
OwnerJoey (operator)
EthicsAligned with [[don't exploit]]

W-2 employees at $25-35/hr with benefits and PTO, vs. the Lowcountry 1099 floor of ~$18/hr with nothing. ~15-25% pricing premium passed through transparently to the customer. Unlocks bid types most outfits can't touch: prevailing-wage public work, school districts, HOA boards that care, churches, contracts that require E-Verify. The premium customer base is also the lowest-haggle and the highest-recurring-referral, so the pricing math survives.

Why this is the foundation: every other product on this page lives on top of livable-wage labor being the brand promise. If the foundation isn't W-2 from Day 1, the “ethical roofing” positioning is marketing not infrastructure, and the corps's [[don't exploit]] rule fails internally. No backdoor, no “just this once,” no contractor-of-contractor shell games.

2. Drone-based honest assessment

PRODUCT · LOW CAPITAL
RevenuePer-assessment, B2C + B2B
CycleYear-round, counter-seasonal
OwnerCorps Infrastructure (Justin tech, Joey calibration)
EthicsClean. No upsell incentive.

Replace the “free inspection that's really a sales pitch” with a paid drone scan, AI damage analysis, standardized condition report the homeowner owns and can show to any roofer for honest competing quotes. Sold to homeowners pre-listing, to insurance adjusters, to property managers, to real estate agents at the inspection cycle. ~$50-200 per assessment depending on roof complexity.

The structural insight: roofing assessments today are subsidized by upsell incentive. Drone + AI lets us decouple assessment from sale. That's a thing nobody offers because nobody in the trade wants to offer it. The corps does, because the corps doesn't need a roof sale to come out of the assessment to break even. Capital required: drone (~$2-5k), software (Justin builds), licensing (FAA Part 107, weekend course).

3. Roof claim navigation kit

KIT · DIGITAL · ZERO SEASONALITY
Revenue$29-49 per kit, one-off
CycleFlat. Spikes post-hurricane.
OwnerJustin (Kit Machine)
EthicsFightAClaim shape. No UPL exposure.

Direct parallel to FightAClaim in the Kit Machine portfolio, applied to roof insurance claims. Customer asserts their own rights against their carrier; demand-letter pattern; safe kit shape per the cron prompt's disqualifier #7. Roof insurance claims are routinely under-scoped, denied, or assigned wrong scope by carrier-friendly adjusters. Homeowners typically lose because they don't know what to ask for. Kit gives them the language, the supplements, and the appeal scripts.

Two distribution paths: standalone kit sold on shinypsyduck.com via the Kit Machine, AND bundled free with every Magnolia replacement (“we just replaced your roof; here's the kit so your next claim doesn't get short-paid”). Bundling creates the brand-on-brand loyalty loop that pure kit-sales doesn't generate.

4. Maintenance subscriptions

RECURRING · LOW CAPITAL
RevenueMonthly recurring, ~$25-75/mo
CycleFlat — same in Feb as July
OwnerJoey (sales), Justin (tech), Crew (delivery)
EthicsClean.

Annual inspection plus minor repair contracts sold to HOAs, churches, commercial property owners, high-end residential. Monthly recurring revenue, real labor obligations (visit on schedule, photograph everything, log all repairs to the ledger). This is the single biggest cyclicality smoother on the labor side. A vertical with 200 maintenance subs at $40/mo is $96k/yr that doesn't care what month it is or what rates are doing.

Why it works for the corps specifically: ledger pays the assigned crew member on the sub for as long as the sub renews. So the roofer who originally sold the sub and did the first 3 inspections keeps earning on it even after they move on to other corps work. Solo roofers don't build this revenue because they can't price-in their future absence. The corps can.

5. Counter-cyclical trades bundle

LABOR · OFF-SEASON
RevenuePer-job, off-season
CyclePeaks Dec-Feb (winter)
OwnerJoey (operator), Corps (training)
EthicsClean. Federal grant alignment.

Insulation, weatherization, gutter and soffit work, attic ventilation, ice and water shield retrofits. Same crew, same trucks, different work order. SC has a state-administered Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP) funded by DOE; the federal IRA also expanded weatherization tax credits through 2032. Winter is when this work peaks (homeowners feel the cold bills, federal disbursements land Q1). Direct counter-cycle to roof replacement.

The crew utilization argument: a roofer who's also a certified weatherization installer hits ~85% calendar utilization vs. the ~55% pure-roofer norm. That's the difference between a crew member taking home $52k/yr and $78k/yr at the same hourly rate. Crew loyalty solves at $78k that doesn't solve at $52k.

6. Solar + roof bundle

LABOR + PARTNER · MID CAPITAL
RevenuePer-bundle, ITC-driven
CycleQ4 spike (ITC), less rate-sensitive
OwnerJoey + solar partner / NABCEP install
EthicsClean. Customer saves money.

Most roofers don't do solar; most solar installers don't do roof prep. Customers end up paying for two trips, two coordination overheads, two warranties that argue with each other when a leak shows up around an array mount. A single-contractor bundle saves the homeowner 15-30% on combined cost and eliminates the warranty-fingerpointing. Federal ITC (30% through 2032) creates Q4 demand spike from homeowners chasing the tax credit before year-end. Less housing-rate-sensitive than re-roof because the payback math survives.

Two paths to capability: NABCEP-certified hire on the roofing crew (12-18 month skill ramp, ~$5-8k training), OR a referral partnership with a local solar installer where Magnolia does roof prep + structural and the partner does PV install. Path 2 ships faster and is less capital-intensive; Path 1 captures more margin and is the right end-state.

What the seven products do together

The portfolio shape matters more than any single product

Seven products with different cycle profiles, different capital requirements, and different owners means the vertical isn't betting on any one of them. Three could underperform and the other four still carry the vertical. The depot could face a 2-year permit fight and the other six still generate revenue. The 2026 housing market could go sideways and the maintenance subs, drone service, and weatherization work still keep the lights on. This is what “corps vertical” means structurally: a portfolio held together by an ethics floor and a shared ledger, not a single shop's P&L.

The Apprentice Track underneath the labor-shaped six

Six of the seven products above are labor-shaped at delivery. None of them are sustainable if the corps can't hire and develop Carolinas workers without burning them out or paying them poverty wages. That makes the apprentice track structural infrastructure, not a side benefit. Three principles:

  • Location-based identity, anchored in Colleton County. Apprentices join the corps, not “a roofing crew.” The program home is Colleton County (Walterboro and surrounds), where Jeremy mentors and the corps's training facility lives. Apprentices identify as Colleton corps apprentices, deployable across verticals. Most US apprenticeship programs are trade-locked; the corps's competitive recruiting advantage is that workers don't have to pick a trade for life at age 19, and the program is rooted in a rural county that has been losing its trade workforce for a decade.
  • Cross-trained by default, staged. Year 1: pick one craft as primary (roofing or marine) and master safety + shared skills across both. Year 2-3: optional shadow rotations in the other vertical, paid. Year 3+: optional dual-craft certification path with a ledger bonus. Most workers will stay single-craft; the ones who go dual hit ~85% calendar utilization and become the corps's deployment-flexibility asset.
  • Year-round employment is the promise. The winter alignment between marine refit (Dec-Feb peak) and roofing dead season is what makes this credible. A dual-craft apprentice does boat decking in winter at Jeremy's Colleton shop, roofing on local Colleton jobs or rotating to Joey's Lowcountry projects in spring through fall, weatherization on weeks where neither is hot. They're a corps member every month of the year. That's a real differentiator in a county where most trade workers face Dec-Feb income gaps.
The depot is not an apprentice site. The shingle recycling depot in Columbia is a processing facility, not craft work. It needs an on-site operator (Joey) plus 1-2 paid workers (forklift, weighbridge, equipment ops). Not part of the apprentice program. Different geography, different function, different people. Keep them separate in any conversation about either.

The shingle recycling depot, the real version

This is the product Justin specifically wants. The progression he named (get land, start as a dump, charge by the load, gather equipment, recycle, ship residuals) is the right one and the economics work. Below is the full sequence with capital, timeline, and risk for each step. Treat this as the spec for a corps Infrastructure-axis asset that, if built, could outlast every single labor-side product on this page.

The one-line pitch

11 million tons per year of asphalt shingle waste goes to US landfills. Roofers pay $300 to $500 per job in disposal fees. SC DOT uses Recycled Asphalt Shingles in road paving and pays for them. The asymmetry is real and uncaptured at the statewide regional level. Build a depot at the Columbia logistics hub (I-26, I-20, I-77 converge there) that serves roofers from Charleston to Charlotte to Greenville to the Lowcountry. Charge roofers to dump. Sell the output to DOT. Ship only the residual material to landfill. Every roof we replace literally becomes a road.

The progression, step by step

1

Acquire land in the Columbia logistics hub

1 to 3 acres of light-industrial-zoned land in Columbia metro outskirts along the I-26 / I-20 / I-77 corridor convergence. Columbia is the only city in SC where three interstates meet, which means a depot there sits within 1.5 hr drive of every major roofing market in the Carolinas: Lowcountry (I-26), Charleston (I-26), Greenville (I-26), Charlotte border (I-77), Florence (I-20), and Augusta (I-20). That's the entire catchment, not just the Beaufort corridor.

Target submarkets: Blythewood, Lugoff, and Kershaw County (rural designation preserves USDA Rural Business Development Grant eligibility), with Calhoun County backup. Avoid Cayce and the urbanized Lexington County areas since they kill USDA eligibility. Industrial land in these submarkets runs $30-100k for 1-3 acres, generally cheaper than Lowcountry industrial land with dramatically better access.

Risk: wrong site within Columbia means highway access becomes a left-turn-onto-state-route nightmare. Mitigation: pick parcels with direct interstate entrance/exit, not parcels that require trucks through residential. Capital: $30-100k. Timeline: 3-6 months.

2

Permits and zoning

SC DHEC permit for C&D (Construction & Demolition) waste recycling. County zoning approval for waste handling at the selected parcel. SCDOT supplier qualification once recycled material is being produced. Stormwater management plan. Possible NPDES permit. This is the slow step; SC DHEC C&D permits commonly take 6-18 months depending on county and site complexity.

Risk: permits stall in a county we picked badly. Mitigation: pre-permit conversations with DHEC and the county before we commit to the land purchase. Capital: $5k-$20k in permit fees and legal. Timeline: 6-18 months, overlapping with steps 1 and 3.

3

Open as a tipping facility

Once permits clear, the depot can operate as a simple paid drop-off: roofers drive up, weighbridge logs the load, they pay by the ton, they leave. $50-100/ton tipping fee (vs. $80-150/ton at the regional landfill). At this stage there's no processing happening; we're just stockpiling shingles in a permitted yard and selling convenience. This is profitable on Day 1 of operation: a depot that does 5 loads/day at $400/load is $500k+ revenue with minimal opex.

Risk: volume doesn't materialize because we underprice landfill or oversell our convenience. Mitigation: pre-sign 5-8 anchor accounts before we open, sourced from Magnolia (immediate), Columbia metro roofers (the largest local pool), and 2-3 Charleston / Greenville / Lowcountry shops willing to use the depot for jobs within trucking range. Capital: weighbridge ($30-60k installed), small office trailer, perimeter fencing, first-90-days operating cost. ~$150k. Timeline: 60 days from permit approval.

4

Acquire processing equipment

Used asphalt shingle grinder/shredder (~$80-150k used, $200-300k new). Magnetic nail separator (~$30-50k). Conveyor system, shaker screen for sizing (~$30-60k). Loader (~$50-80k used). This phase is “buy slowly as cash from tipping fees and tax-strategic financing allow.” The depot stays open as a tipping facility during equipment build-out; equipment comes online as it's purchased. Probably 12-24 months of progressive acquisition.

Risk: equipment underperforms on the mix of regional shingles received (varying organic content, coastal moisture in Lowcountry deliveries, granular loss differences across age and brand). Mitigation: bench-test with one used grinder before committing to the production fleet. Capital: $200-400k phased over 18-24 months. Timeline: overlaps with tipping operation.

5

Begin RAS production and sales

Once the production line is operational, shingles in the yard become Recycled Asphalt Shingles (RAS) sold to paving companies and SC DOT. SC DOT specs RAS use up to 5% by weight in asphalt mixes; some states allow up to 20%. SC DOT's District 5 (Lowcountry) and District 6 (Charleston) are the natural buyers. Pricing: ~$10-40/ton sold (variable), at ~30-40% margin after processing energy. Layered on top of the existing tipping revenue, RAS sales double or triple revenue per ton received.

Risk: SC DOT spec changes or competing supplier undercuts. Mitigation: qualify with multiple private paving companies in parallel with DOT. Capital: minimal incremental; just sales+logistics labor. Timeline: 6 months from equipment online.

6

Ship only the residuals

Underlayment, debris, contaminated material, and material that fails the processing line still has to go to landfill. Real number is typically 10-15% of incoming weight ships out as actual residual waste. We pay landfill tipping fees on that small fraction only. That's the move: convert “100% of incoming material is landfill cost” (the roofer's current situation) to “10-15% of incoming material is landfill cost, the other 85-90% is RAS revenue” (our situation).

Risk: residual percentage creeps higher than projected. Mitigation: incoming-load inspection and tipping-fee differential for high-debris loads. Capital: rolling op cost. Timeline: steady-state by month 24-36.

How the corps absorbs roofing's seasonality and housing dependency

Justin's concern is real and important. Roofing is one of the most cyclical residential trades there is. Two cycles stacked: seasonal (Dec-Feb dead, summer peaks but heat caps it) and macro (re-roof correlates with home equity, refi activity, and rates). 2022-2023 hammered a lot of Carolina roofers when rates spiked. A solo shop eats both cycles. A corps vertical absorbs them through three structural layers.

Layer 1 · Counter-cyclical labor

Trades the same crew can run when roofs aren't happening

Marine winter refit (cross-trained members only): the cleanest alignment of any option on this list. Dec-Feb is peak indoor refit at Jeremy's shop because owners want boats ready for spring launch. Same months are roofing-dead. A cross-trained corps member can do EVA decking, dash panels, and hull work during the exact weeks they couldn't do roofs. Only works inside the corps structure, since solo trades can't legally share a workforce across separate businesses.

Insulation and weatherization: winter peaks. SC has a Weatherization Assistance Program; federal IRA expanded weatherization credits through 2032. Same crew, same trucks. Direct counter-cycle.

Gutter and soffit work: quicker, lower weather dependence than tear-off. Winter-friendly.

Solar bundle: Q4 demand spike from homeowners chasing the ITC tax credit before year-end. Less weather-sensitive than roof replacement, and the payback math survives rate environments that kill discretionary home spend.

Commercial roofing: different bid cycle, off-season scheduling (work happens when tenants are out), municipal and school district RFPs hit in fall.

Layer 2 · Recurring or asset-tied revenue

Revenue layered on the labor work that pays regardless of install cycle

Maintenance subscriptions: monthly recurring. Same in Feb as July. 200 subs at $40/mo = $96k/yr smoothed.

Shingle recycling depot: runs year-round even when Magnolia's own installs slow. Other roofers' demo waste, commercial tear-offs, storm cleanup keep the depot fed off-cycle.

Drone assessment service: sold to insurance adjusters, property managers, real estate inspectors. Year-round demand.

Roof claim kit: digital. Zero seasonality. Spikes post-hurricane.

Layer 3 · The corps ledger

Personal income smoothing through paid-on-past-products

This is the structural answer most people miss. The corps ledger pays members on past products for as long as the products sell. Someone who shipped 10 maintenance contracts in October keeps earning from them in February even when they aren't actively installing. Someone who built the depot in year 1 earns on it in year 5 when residential installs are down 40%.

A solo roofer can't do this; their income is exactly their current labor. A corps vertical can, because the products outlive the labor cycle. That's why “corps vertical” isn't just “multi-product LLC”: the ledger structure converts cyclical labor income into smoother product income at the member level.

The cross-vertical bonus with Jeremy

Boat refit season IS roofing dead season. Winter aligns perfectly.

The textbook counter-cyclical pairing the corps actually unlocks: major marine refit work peaks December through February because boat owners want their decks, hulls, and mechanicals done before spring launch. Roofing dies in those same months. Same calendar window kills roof crews and runs Jeremy's shop at full capacity. Cross-trained corps members swing from roofing November-March to marine refit December-February to roofing March-October. ~85% calendar utilization vs. the ~55% pure-single-trade norm.

Why this order and not faster

The temptation is to have all the conversations at once and parallelize the build. That's the wrong move for two reasons. First, going to Joey before the corps exists puts the conversation in hypothetical-mode and the answer comes back in hypothetical-mode (“maybe, send me what you build”). Going to Joey after a working corps with a real first product puts the conversation in concrete-mode (“here's the ledger, here's last month's payouts, here's the roofing-vertical roadmap”). The answer-quality is dramatically different.

Second, the greenfield window is real but not as tight as we might tell ourselves. Joey is “expanding into the Lowcountry” in the sense of marketing and intent, not in the sense of having signed a 100-employee lease and a $400k disposal contract. The actual operational commitments are probably still 90-180 days out. We have enough time to get the corps built, ship one product, and bring Joey a real story.

Psyduck's honest read

This is the most ambitious section of the corps roadmap, and the highest upside

Seven product lines, three layers of smoothing, a depot that could outlive every shop on this hub. The mutual aid pattern (Joey to Jeremy on ops, Jeremy to Joey on geography, Justin to all on tech) is itself a strong reason for the corps structure: none of these three could deliver this much value to each other through any normal LLC arrangement, and a corps with two verticals and three contributing founders is structurally much harder to dismiss than what the corps looked like a week ago.

Probability-weighted outcomes for the full vertical

  • ~30% chance: all 7 lines ship within 36 months, depot operational at run-rate. This is the Joey-as-Co-founder world.
  • ~50% chance: 3-5 lines ship within 36 months, depot doesn't happen or is on a 5-year horizon. This is the Joey-as-Member world.
  • ~15% chance: 2-3 lines ship (kit, drone, maybe subs through a Magnolia partnership). This is the Joey-as-Customer-zero or Joey-as-Ally world.
  • ~5% chance: nothing in this page ships because Jeremy or Joey says no in a way that makes the corps not happen, or the corps forms but never goes beyond marine.

The risks I'm naming, not soft-pedaling

The depot permitting risk is real and asymmetric. $100-200k could go into pre-construction (land deposit, surveys, permit fees, legal) and come back as a denial. The mitigation is staging: don't close on land until DHEC says permittable in writing. We can spend $5-15k investigating without committing to the $50-150k land buy.
The Joey contingency is the single most binary variable on this page. If Joey's answer is Ally, we lose access to ~80% of the upside on this page and the vertical is thin. We have to be okay with that thin version being the realistic outcome and design the corps to be viable on it.
The focus penalty from building two verticals in parallel is real. Marine and roofing both need Justin's tech. The corps's ledger and member-onboarding infrastructure has to ship for either vertical to be a real corps product. We're committing Justin to a multi-year build, which is the kind of commitment burnt-out people don't make safely.
The greenfield window isn't infinite. If we wait more than 9-12 months from now to bring Joey the conversation, his Lowcountry expansion has happened the normal way and we're back to retrofitting an existing operation, which is the wrong shape.

What the vertical does for the three founders, said plainly

This is what pays our retirement, if it works

Two income streams, one of them passive. Same person, different structural outcome.

Every roof we replace becomes a road. Every shingle we don't ship to a landfill is a Connolley promise honored. The corps was supposed to be a 1933 echo. It might also be the thing that pays our retirement.
