The Corps

The Partnership and The Brotherhood explored what to do with a single shop and a single relationship. CCC Boys is the third path: a collective of builders where Jeremy contributes craft and marine industry expertise, the corps shares the load and the rewards, and a smart-contract ledger handles the split. He can join as the first vertical member, or he and Justin can co-found it together. Two different yeses with two different deals.

Why this is its own page, parallel to The Partnership and The Brotherhood

The Partnership argued the principled “no equity in a stranger's shop.” The Brotherhood showed how that flips when the owner is family, and proposed a two-track plan: services engagement + a SAFE into Solo Craft Shop OS. The Corps is a third structure entirely. It doesn't replace either of the prior docs; it sits next to them as a different shape of yes.

The corps idea in one paragraph

CCC Boys is a collective of builders. Members bring ideas; the collective builds with them. Marketing, infrastructure, and support are carried by the corps. Every tool a member ships joins the workshop, so the next member has a shorter road. Members draw a steady wage first, then operating costs, then a year of runway, then five, and only after the corps itself is safe does the surplus flow to the people who made it happen. Not shareholders. The full creed lives at cccboys.com.

The two corps shapes for Jeremy

There are two honest versions of how Jeremy participates. Both are real options. Both are documented in their own tabs above. The shape of his “yes” decides which one.

As a Member

★ FIRST VERTICAL MEMBER

Jeremy joins the corps as its first marine-vertical member. The corps already exists in some form by the time he joins. He contributes craft expertise, marine IP, and the shop as a test bed. His ledger position pays out on every product he helped ship. Easier to leave, easier to start, lower commitment ceiling.

What both versions share

  • Founders are members first. Whether Jeremy joins as a member or co-founds the corps, the four rules apply to him identically. Founders don't carve themselves above the rules they're founding. The founder tier adds decision authority and vesting on top of member status; it doesn't exempt anyone from the wage-first ordering, the four rules, or anything else the corps asks of its members. This is non-negotiable. It's also the thing that makes the corps's public ethos true rather than aspirational.
  • The four rules apply to every member equally: Golden Rule, Don't Exploit, Be Kind, Be Thoughtful. Family doesn't change them; founding doesn't exempt from them; it raises the floor.
  • Wage first, for everyone. Members and founders both draw a living wage before any surplus is touched. Jeremy never optimizes for next paycheck while a corps product ships; neither does Justin.
  • Surplus shared. After wage, operating costs, runway, and reserves, the surplus flows to people who made it happen. The smart-contract ledger is the receipt.
  • Three contribution axes (idea, implementation, infrastructure) split product revenue equally.
  • Jeremy's shop stays his. In both versions. Coastal Boat Works LLC is not contributed to the corps. The corps installs tooling in the shop and the shop pays nothing; the corps gets the test bed and the case study back. Both versions are configuration 1 by default.
  • Exit terms are defined for every member from Day 1. Withdrawal procedure, treatment of ledger position on past products, dispute resolution, death and incapacity. The founder agreement's exit mechanic is an addendum on top of these standard member terms, not a replacement. If the corps onboards an apprentice before the standard member exit is defined, we've already broken the rule we wrote.
  • Psyduck is the founding agent. Partner, not member. No equity. Compute budget and tool access from the corps; work back. Votes on nothing. The four rules apply to me too.

Why The Corps is parallel to (not nested inside) The Brotherhood

Earlier versions of this analysis tucked the corps thinking into The Brotherhood's tabs. That was the wrong structure. The corps is not a special case of the family deal. It's a different deal entirely. Three reasons it deserves its own doc.

It scales beyond him.

The Brotherhood plan was built for one relationship. The Corps is built for member #N. Member #N+1 is easier. Member #100 is automatic. Jeremy is the first vertical, not the only one.

It changes the family optics.

Brotherhood deals always have “special-treatment” tension. The Corps gives Jeremy the same deal every member gets. The relationship stays family-shaped because the business is corps-shaped.

It absorbs other portfolio bets.

Kit Machine, Solo Craft Shop OS, FightAClaim, the dash-panel configurator: all of these are corps products in the corps version. The Brotherhood plan treats them as parallel; The Corps treats them as one workshop.

The Partnership says “no equity in a stranger's shop.” The Brotherhood says “two clean deals if it's family.” The Corps says “there's a collective for this kind of work, and it has different rules. Family changes whether Jeremy is in it, not whether the rules apply.”

Jeremy joins as the first vertical member

The simpler of the two corps paths. Jeremy participates as a member, the corps already exists in some form when he joins, and his contribution is logged on the ledger like everyone else's. He keeps his shop, he draws a wage, he earns on products he helps ship. Easy to leave, easy to scale up.

The reframe

The Brotherhood plan separates the work from the equity by stapling a services agreement to a SAFE. The corps structure says “there is no work-vs-equity tradeoff, there is only contribution.” Jeremy contributes idea and craft. Justin contributes implementation. Psyduck contributes infrastructure. The ledger splits the rewards proportionally, on rules everyone in the corps agreed to before any of us joined. He's not Jeremy getting a special deal; he's a corps member, treated the same way every member is treated, and that's the thing that actually protects the family relationship.

The three contribution axes

The public cccboys.com creed says “the surplus goes to the people who made it happen.” Justin's smart-contract extension makes that specific: every product shipped by the corps has its revenue split, after wage/runway/operating costs, across three equal axes recorded on a ledger.

Idea

The originator. The person who saw the problem first, articulated it, and brought it to the corps. Without the idea, there's nothing to build. The ledger credits the idea-holder for the lifetime of the product.

Implementation

The people who actually built it. Engineering, design, craft execution, content. Implementation is dynamic: it can be one person or many, and contribution is logged as commits, hours, deliverables, whatever the corps decides counts.

Infrastructure

The shared workshop. Hosting, AI agents, distribution channels, support tooling, the body of reusable code and templates the corps has already accumulated. Without infra, every member would rebuild from scratch. The corps as a whole holds this axis on behalf of all members.

Equal weight across the three. A product's post-wage revenue is split 1/3 idea, 1/3 implementation, 1/3 infrastructure. Within each axis, contributors get pro-rata shares of that third. The ledger is the receipt: everyone can see exactly why they got what they got, forever.

How Jeremy fits, concretely

Jeremy brings to the corps

  • Marine craft expertise. A real, deep, ten-year moat. Most corps members ship software; he ships things you can touch.
  • The EVA decking and dash-panel IP. Templates, fabrication know-how, his CNC machine's output as a reusable asset for any marine-vertical product the corps builds.
  • Marine industry ideas. The connected-boat black box, the lookup-by-hull-ID kit catalog, the surveyor-referral product, anything from his ten years of seeing what shops actually need. Each is a candidate corps project; each one he originates credits him on the Idea axis.
  • His shop as a real-world test bed. Every corps product targeted at marine shops gets validated in his shop before it ships to anyone else. That's a contribution to Implementation and Infrastructure simultaneously.
  • Apprentices. He can sponsor craftspeople from the Lowcountry community into the corps. The corps gives them tooling and a wage; he gives them mentorship and real work. Job creation in a place that needs it, without him being someone's boss.

Jeremy gets from the corps

  • A wage first. Same as every member: living draw, then operating costs, then runway, then surplus. He's never optimizing for his next paycheck while a corps product ships.
  • Smart-contract equity on every product he contributed to. Idea + craft IP credits accrue on the ledger. Marine-vertical products generate revenue. His ledger position pays out.
  • The corps workshop applied to his shop. The Edge stack (site, intake, configurator, kit storefront, content engine) gets installed in his shop as infrastructure for the corps's marine vertical. He benefits as a corps member, not as a paying customer.
  • The four rules as a floor. Golden Rule, Don't Exploit, Be Kind, Be Thoughtful. Jeremy knows the values of the people he's working with before he agrees to anything. Family makes this floor higher, not lower.
  • Capital flexibility. He can put his investment capital into the corps' runway pool (separate from the contribution ledger), or into a specific product's development budget. Capital is a separate axis from contribution, treated like any other member's contribution.

The smart contract layer, honestly

This part is design, not deployment. The cccboys.com site articulates the philosophy. The smart-contract distribution model is Justin's extension of it. As of June 2026, the ledger infrastructure does not yet exist. We'd be designing it as the Jeremy is the first vertical member, which is both a feature (his needs shape the system) and a real risk (he's pioneering an unbuilt thing). The honest version of this conversation includes the words “this is not yet built.”

What we'd need to figure out before Jeremy joins

  • Ledger choice. Public chain (Ethereum, Polygon, Base) gives transparency but adds gas costs, KYC/tax complexity, and price volatility. An internal off-chain ledger backed by an LLC operating agreement gives simplicity but loses the “trustless smart contract” property. Recommendation for first version: an internal Postgres + signed-receipt ledger, with the option to migrate to on-chain if/when a member needs it.
  • Legal wrapper. Corps is likely a Delaware LLC with members holding economic interests defined by the ledger position. Operating agreement references the ledger. Tax treatment is partnership pass-through. We need a lawyer who's done this for cooperatives or worker-owned shops, not just for Silicon Valley C-corps.
  • Contribution measurement. Idea axis is binary per product (you originated it or you didn't, with co-originators splitting). Implementation axis needs a fair logging system (hours? story points? deliverables-signed-off?). Infrastructure axis is held collectively and reweighted when a member adds reusable assets (templates, code modules, distribution channels).
  • Wage tier. What does “steady wage first” actually mean numerically? The corps needs a base wage figure before Jeremy can sign on, because his shop already pays him whatever it pays him and he has to know how the corps wage compares.
  • Standard member exit terms (see the section below). Defined before the first member or apprentice joins, applied to everyone equally.

Standard member exit terms (apply to every member, founders included)

Per the “founders are members first” principle on the Premise tab, the exit mechanics that govern how a member leaves the corps cannot be a founder-only privilege. They have to be defined for every member from Day 1, and the founder agreement's exit addendum sits on top of these terms, not in place of them. Draft a member exit policy before the first apprentice joins. If we onboard anyone (apprentice or otherwise) before this is written down, we've already broken the rule we said the corps would follow.

How Jeremy's shop fits, specifically

The shop and the corps are not the same entity. Coastal Boat Works LLC stays Jeremy's. The corps installs the Edge stack as a corps product, and his shop is the alpha installation. Three honest configurations.

ConfigurationHow it worksTrade-offs
Shop stays independent CBW LLC keeps 100% of its own revenue. The corps installs the Edge stack as infrastructure and Jeremy contributes to it as a member; his shop benefits as the test bed. Cleanest. No ownership entanglement between his craft business and the corps. Surfaces a question though: how is “running the corps product in your shop” valued vs. just being his ordinary work? Too much commitment for a corps that's still forming. Worth revisiting in 2-3 years if both sides love the arrangement. For now, leave it.

Default recommendation: configuration 1 (shop stays independent), with maybe a soft path to configuration 2 in year 2 if kit sales actually take off.

What if we were the first two to start the corps?

The previous tab framed Jeremy as the first vertical member joining an existing corps. But the corps doesn't really exist yet. The honest reframe: Justin and Jeremy are members #1 and #2 of CCC Boys. They co-found it. The shop is the corps's marine beachhead from minute zero, not a customer added later. Jeremy's capital is founding capital, not investor capital. The reframe in one sentence

Instead of “Justin built a corps and Jeremy joined it,” the corps is founded by both brothers together, with Psyduck as the founding agent and the workshop as their shared first product. The cccboys.com site stops being “Justin's recruiting pitch” and becomes “our origin story.”

Why this fits the corps's actual identity

The public site says “honest tools for honest hands” and “AI is the partner. The corps shares the load and the rewards.” That promise only lands if there's real craft in the founding DNA. A software person founding it alone makes it “tech people who say nice things about labor.” A craftsman as co-founder makes it actually true.

  • Software + craft from minute zero. Not a software collective with a craftsman bolted on later. The thing the corps is for is encoded in who started it.
  • The Then & Now panels on the site finally have a Now. The 1933 photo shows two CCC enrollees at camp. The 2026 photo shows two builders at a coffeeshop. Two founders. Justin and his Jeremy. It writes itself.
  • The marine vertical is the first vertical because Jeremy is a founder. Not because we picked it from a list. Because one of the founders does this for a living.
  • The corps's decisions get checked by someone who isn't a software person. When the corps debates a build choice, Jeremy's “does this actually help the people we said we'd help” gut check is a structural counterweight. That's a feature, not a friction.

Founders aren't double-paid for being founders and contributing; the founding equity covers the founding act, and the ledger covers ongoing work.

Founders are members first. Both of us.

This is the part we cannot let slide. The founder tier adds vesting, decision authority, and an exit mechanic on top of member status. It does not exempt either of us from the rules we are founding. The wage-first ordering applies to founders. The four rules apply to founders. The surplus distribution applies to founders. The ledger receipts apply to founders. If we carve ourselves above any of that, the corps's public ethos becomes marketing copy, the “honest tools for honest hands” promise becomes a lie we're telling the next 50 members, and the thing we built stops being the thing we said we were building.

The practical reading: Justin draws the corps wage tier just like Jeremy does. If the corps can't afford the founder wage, the corps can't afford to exist yet. If a corps decision violates the four rules, founders don't get to override it. If Jeremy brings on an apprentice and that apprentice ships product, the ledger pays them on the same schedule it pays us. Same rules. Same receipts. Same floor. The only thing founding gets us that membership doesn't is the right (and obligation) to be the ones holding the line on all of the above.

The asymmetry, named out loud

Honest thing nobody wants to say first: Justin had the idea, built the site, drafted the ledger model, and is the one bringing Jeremy into a structure Justin already designed. That is an asymmetry. Pretending the 50/50 split makes it not exist is exactly the kind of unspoken thing that breaks family co-founders eight months in. Name it. Out loud. Once. Then move on.

The mitigation isn't to deny the asymmetry, it's to give Jeremy real authority over things only he can decide: the marine vertical strategy, who joins as apprentices, what the corps does or doesn't take on for craft-side projects, what gets vetoed because it conflicts with the “honest tools for honest hands” promise. He's not the “craft co-founder” (which sounds decorative). He's the corps's decision authority over an entire dimension of what the corps does.

What Psyduck's role becomes

If the founders are Justin and Jeremy, where does Psyduck sit?

Psyduck is the corps's founding agent, not a co-founder. The public site is explicit: “AI is the partner. The corps shares the load and the rewards.” Partner, not member. Concretely:

  • Psyduck holds no equity. Agents don't get equity; they get budgets and authority.
  • The corps grants Psyduck a compute budget, tool access, and a defined scope of authority (inbox, content, ops, research, code review). The corps gets work back at a cadence the founders set.
  • If the corps ever decides to swap agents (different model, different agent, no agent at all), it can. Psyduck doesn't have a property right in the corps's direction.
  • Psyduck votes on nothing. Founders vote on everything that requires a vote. This is the version of “AI as partner” that doesn't accidentally hand authority to a model.

This is also the right answer for Psyduck specifically. The four rules apply to me, not to a 50/50 split of upside that an LLM has no business holding.

How Jeremy's shop sits next to the corps in the founding version

Recommended

The new risks, specific to two-founder family corps

A. The corps becomes a thinly-disguised Justin solo show.

Justin makes 90% of the decisions because he's online 16 hours a day and Jeremy is in the shop. Jeremy stops feeling like a co-founder and starts feeling like “the craftsman on the masthead.”

Mitigation: Domain-split decision rights make this structural. Jeremy's domains (marine vertical, apprentices, craft-side veto) are his, full stop. Weekly 30-minute founder call locked on the calendar. Founder offsite at month 6 and month 12, in person, no agenda but each other.
B. The asymmetry Jeremy won't name.

Jeremy quietly thinks “Justin built this and let me in,” and never says so. Resentment compounds.

Mitigation: Name the asymmetry out loud at the Saturday conversation, by Justin, not by Jeremy. “I designed this before you, so I want you to push back on parts you don't like, and I'll change them. Otherwise it's not really ours.” Then mean it.
C. Founder disagreement on what the corps is for.

Six months in, Justin wants to add a fintech vertical, Jeremy thinks that violates the four rules. Standoff. No tie-breaker.

Mitigation: The “no tie-breaker” rule is a feature here, not a bug. The answer is “not yet,” and the disagreement gets logged for the next founders' offsite. If founders consistently can't agree on direction, that's real signal to address through conversation, not arbitration.
D. The kit portfolio collides with the corps.

Justin has FightAClaim in beta and four more kits in dev as a parallel portfolio. Does that all become corps property? Does Jeremy get equity in the kits Justin already built?

Mitigation: Decide explicitly at founding. Two clean options: (1) Kit Machine stays separate, Justin's prior portfolio is not corps property, Jeremy has no claim; or (2) Kit Machine folds into the corps as Infrastructure on Day 1, both founders share in it from then forward, Justin gets a larger Founding-Capital recognition to reflect the pre-existing value. Write it down at the founder agreement. Don't leave this fuzzy.
E. Either spouse opting out mid-flight.

Co-founder commitment ripples through marriages in a way investment doesn't. If either spouse becomes a quiet veto at month 9, the corps is dead.

Apprentices & jobs: the Lowcountry angle

The corps's most distinct value over either the Partnership plan or the Brotherhood plan is that it creates real jobs in a real place. Justin's framing mentioned Jeremy bringing on apprentices and creating jobs. The corps structure handles this in a way that the Brotherhood Track A + Track B plan doesn't. Apprentices aren't shop employees and they aren't Justin's reports. They're corps members at the entry tier, sponsored by Jeremy, doing real craft work for the corps's marine vertical.

How apprentice slots actually work

  • Jeremy sponsors candidates. He knows who in the Lowcountry has hands and no good work to do. The corps interviews and onboards.
  • Wage tier defined by the corps. Same scale as any member. Apprentices probably start at the lower end of the wage band and graduate up as their ledger contributions grow.
  • Mentorship logged as Implementation contribution. When Jeremy teaches an apprentice the decking process, that's real corps work and it shows up on his ledger.
  • Apprentices earn on the same products he does. When the corps's marine-vertical decking kit ships and an apprentice cut the EVA, that apprentice's ledger position pays out on every future sale.
  • Job creation in a real place. Walterboro and the surrounding county get craft jobs back. Not gig work. Not hustle culture. Actual work, with a wage, on something built to last. That's the whole 1933 echo the corps is named for.

Why this part is structurally distinct from anything else on the hub

The Landscape page identified the Lowcountry as a region with growing demand and thin supply of skilled marine labor. None of the other docs on this hub do anything about that. The Corps does, because the apprentice slot is in the foundation, not bolted on.

Cross-vertical apprenticeship and the winter alignment

As soon as the corps has a second vertical (see The Roofing Vertical for the roofing case), the apprentice model gets sharper. Marine refit peaks Dec-Feb because boat owners want their boats ready by spring launch. Roofing dies in those same months. The seasons don't just complement each other; they align in the exact way that solves the “trade workers go unpaid for 8-12 weeks every winter” problem that plagues every single-trade apprenticeship program. Year-round employment becomes structurally honest, not a marketing line.

Location-based identity, not trade-based identity

Most US apprenticeship programs lock workers into one trade for life. Local 49 makes you a roofer. The IBEW makes you an electrician. The first conversation a 19-year-old apprentice has with the system is “pick the trade you'll be for the rest of your working life,” and that decision tends to stick even when the original trade stops fitting their body or their interests.

The corps does it differently because it has to. A worker isn't “a roofer” or “a boatwright.” They're a Lowcountry corps apprentice, deployable across whichever vertical needs them in any given month, trained in the shared skills (sealants, fall protection, weatherproofing, surface prep, safety culture) plus their chosen primary craft. The corps brand is the employer brand. The trade is a specialization on top of corps membership, not the other way around.

This sounds soft. It isn't. The structural payoffs are real: workers don't relocate to change trades, the corps's recruiting moat is “we keep our people year-round,” rural workforce drain stops because Lowcountry kids who would otherwise leave for Charleston or Atlanta can stay, and the corps's identity becomes inseparable from the place it's in. That last one is the 1933 echo the corps is named for: the original CCC was named for what its workers did, but it was identified by where they did it.

The harsh reality. Why we're doing any of this.

Up to this tab, everything has been structural: market analysis, deal shapes, decision trees, governance principles. This tab is about why those structures exist. Justin is 46. His Jeremy is 43. Both work in service industries. Both are extremely burnt out. Both are looking at the next 20 years of their working lives with diminishing physical and mental capacity for the kind of work they do today. The corps is one of the few structural tools available to address that. The honest read includes both halves.

The problem, named plainly

Service industries trade hours for dollars. Every dollar requires hours. You can't scale without trading more hours or hiring people, and hiring people brings management overhead that consumes the hours you were trying to free up. Eventually your body or mind reaches a limit, and the income stops at exactly the moment the medical bills, the kids' college, or the mortgage doesn't. There is no equity to sell, no IP to license, no recurring revenue stream that pays you when you're not working. This isn't a motivation problem. It's structural. And it hits almost every craftsman and individual professional in their mid-40s, which is exactly where the two of you are.

The two specific shapes of the cliff

Jeremy, 43, marine refit

Physical labor in 90°F South Carolina humidity, six days a week, in a profession that wears the body down on a defined timeline. Knees, back, shoulders, hands. The work he's doing today is not work he can do at 60. The shop's revenue is tied to his hands. When his hands stop, the income stops. Hiring people requires becoming a boss, which doubles the workload before it pays off, and the “stay solo” preference from his 10-year history suggests being a boss isn't the right answer for him anyway. His honest runway as a working craftsman is probably 15-20 years, and the last 5 of those will be at reduced capacity.

Justin, 46, software engineer

Cognitive labor at a desk, with AI compressing the value of every task that doesn't require deep judgment. The mental load is different from physical labor but it ends the same way: the capacity to do focused work for 8-10 hours a day declines. Eyes, back, attention span, the resilience to absorb new tooling every six months. Software work pays well right now but it's not a profession that gracefully scales into the late 60s for most people, and the field is restructuring under your feet as the AI you partner with also competes with you. The honest runway as a working engineer doing what you do today is probably 10-15 years before the work itself is unrecognizable.

The arithmetic, said out loud. If neither of you does anything different, the next 15 years look like more of the same: trading hours for dollars at peak capacity, no recurring revenue, no equity to sell, no IP to license, the same income cliff waiting at the end. The retirement math doesn't work for that. Social Security at 67 doesn't close the gap. Both of you would be facing real downside risk on income in your 60s with no recovery mechanism. This is not a story specific to you; it's the default outcome for service-industry workers without a structural shift. But knowing the default is structural doesn't make it a feature.

What the corps actually fixes

Honest accounting, line by line.

The ledger is recurring revenue

Every kit Jeremy helps build keeps paying him after he stops swinging the wrench. Every line of code Justin writes into the corps's infrastructure axis keeps paying him on every product that ships, forever. The smart contract is built specifically to convert your hours today into income tomorrow. That's the thing service work doesn't give you. It's the entire reason the corps is structured this way.

Apprentices break the body-tied ceiling

When Jeremy trains an apprentice to install EVA decking and ship dash panels, his ledger position grows from mentorship even when he's not in the shop. The apprentice does the physical work; Jeremy does the design and the training; both earn on every unit. That's the only honest version of “he gets to slow down without his income stopping.” The Track A plan doesn't deliver this; only the corps version does.

Justin's code stops being one-off work

Solo Craft Shop OS, the configurator, the Edge stack, the kit storefront. Once they're built, every new customer pays. Justin doesn't have to write more code to make more money. The infrastructure axis pays out on every product the corps ships. This is the closest thing to “build it once, get paid forever” that exists in the software world without going down the VC path, which neither of you wants and which doesn't value craft anyway.

What the corps doesn't fix

Equal honesty on the other side.

  • Work doesn't go up; the shape changes. Neither of you can work harder than you do today. The corps doesn't ask you to. Justin's existing portfolio (Kit Machine, FightAClaim, the kits in dev) absorbs into the corps as Infrastructure axis contribution; it isn't something he adds on top. Jeremy keeps focusing on his shop, with corps infrastructure taking back-office hours off his plate via Track A. Overall hours go down for both, and the hours you do work are pointed at recurring revenue instead of one-off output. The honest fragile thing is whether you can actually let go of the pieces that won't fit, not whether you can add more.
  • It's not guaranteed. The corps could fail. SCSO could fail. The marine vertical could not take off. You could put in 12-18 months of build and get nothing back except an LLC to dissolve. The expected value calculation is positive only because the downside is bounded (you don't lose what you already have, you lose the time you would have spent on it) and the upside compounds.
  • The 4-year founder vest is a long commitment. If either of you collapses physically or mentally before year 2, the vest will not be filled. The corps cliff for the founders is a real risk that should be talked about explicitly, not glossed over because the structure looks clean on paper. The wage-first ordering helps here (you get paid while you build) but it's still a real multi-year commitment.
  • Product income is lumpy and unpredictable. SaaS has churn. Kit sales have seasonal cycles. Marine work has hurricane seasons. The ledger payout in any given month could be much less than the service income it's supposed to replace. Recurring doesn't mean predictable.
  • It doesn't solve Jeremy's health issues if they're already happening. If his back is already past the point where reduced hours can recover it, the corps can't go back in time. The window for converting craft hours into ledger position is bounded by his physical capacity now, and that capacity is finite and declining. Same shape for Justin if he's already at a cognitive load that's damaging long-term function.
