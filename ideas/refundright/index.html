PASS · ETHICS FAIL · CHARGING VICTIMS · DOT ENFORCEMENT SOFTENED
THE GAP (WAS REAL)

The DOT's 2024 final rule (effective October 28, 2024) mandates automatic cash refunds within 7 business days for canceled or significantly delayed flights. Airlines are still routinely offering travel credits instead — DOT has already secured $4B+ in enforcement actions confirming non-compliance is widespread.

Every major flight compensation service (AirHelp, Flightright, ClaimCompass, FlightRefunder) is strictly EU261/UK261 — they require European law, fixed compensation tables, and no-win-no-fee contingency. Zero players have built a dedicated US DOT refund enforcement product. The $19 one-time kit gap was structurally real and competitively unoccupied.

The chargeback guide (document #3 in the kit) would have been the most durable piece — bank enforcement via Reg Z operates independently of DOT posture. Even if enforcement softens further, the chargeback lever stays live.

WHY WE PASSED
ETHICS FAILURE — CHARGING VICTIMS TWICE — The airline already wronged the traveler. Charging them $20 to fight back means they're paying again to recover money they never should have lost. We profit whether or not they get their refund. The EU261 contingency model (no-win-no-fee) is ethically coherent — a flat upfront fee kit is not. This is the same pattern as PIPKit: the paying customer and the victim are the same person.
REVENUE FLOWS FROM VICTIMS, NOT TO THEM — Our ethics rule is clear: we don't build businesses where the paying customer is the person being hurt by a broken system. A traveler who just had their flight canceled and was denied a cash refund is in that category. $20 from someone who's already out $300+ is hitting them while they're down.
DOT ENFORCEMENT POSTURE SOFTENED — The Trump DOT under Sean Duffy has been deregulatory. Consumer protection guidance pages are 404ing. The demand letter threat carries less immediate sting without active enforcement. The rule is still on the books, but airlines know the regulator is less likely to act. This doesn't kill the kit, but it weakens the product's strongest lever (DOT complaint threat).
THE "MAYBE" IS DOING TOO MUCH WORK — Spend $20 to MAYBE get back money you were already owed. The customer is paying for uncertain recovery of money that should have been automatic. That framing is hard to sell ethically and practically.
THE LESSON

A real gap + a real legal right + a real community ≠ a business we should build. The ethics question is independent of the market question. RefundRight would have cleared the competitive vet, the legal vet, and the SEO analysis — but it fails the foundational rule before any of that matters.

The key distinction: ContractorClaim (homeowner suing a contractor who stiffed them) and FeeBack (car buyer disputing unauthorized add-ons) passed because the customer is asserting a right against a third-party wrongdoer — and winning is highly probable (contractor took the money, the fee is on the window sticker). RefundRight fails because the outcome is genuinely uncertain ($20 to MAYBE recover the money) and the airline already wronged them once. We'd be a second charge on top of the first harm.

The viable version: A contingency model — free kit, we take 15% if the chargeback succeeds. That's aligned. But it requires being a financial intermediary, which is a different legal structure entirely.

THE VERDICT
PASS · ETHICS FAIL · CHARGING VICTIMS

The gap was real. We're not the right seller for this product. The DOT's 2024 refund rule created a genuine enforcement vacuum that no one is monetizing. But "no one is charging victims for this yet" is not a business opportunity — it's an indictment of the model.

Score: 2.5/10. Above zero because the gap and legal foundation are solid and the chargeback layer is genuinely useful. But any score above zero here is misleading — we passed on ethics, not viability. The competitive moat doesn't matter if we can't clear the ethics bar.

If the model were contingency — take a cut only when the traveler wins — it would be a different conversation. That version aligns our incentives with the customer's outcome. The flat-fee version does not.
